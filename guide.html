Radar Explorer Guide

A reference manual for the 3D NEXRAD explorer: how to drive it, how the radar and the code turn pulses into a volume, how to read each field, and, just as important, what the radar cannot see. Written for use on Puget Sound and the Salish Sea first, with the Big Island and Houston alongside.

What this is, and is not

The explorer runs entirely in your browser. In live mode it reads NOAA's public data buckets directly, decodes each radar volume, and grids it into 3D on your own machine. Past cases were built ahead of time in Python. Nothing here is an official product or a warning service; the NWS, NHC/CPHC and the Coast Guard are. The marine chart layer is not for navigation.

1Using it quickly

The picture fills the screen. Controls sit in a panel (left on a wide screen, a bottom sheet on a phone) with six tabs. The time bar runs along the bottom, the colour key sits at a corner, and a dock opens below the scene for 2D charts: cross-section, QVP, winds and Skew-T.

Views

A view is a centre (the whole grid, the focus, a radar, or a named place), how much ground to fit in the visible part of the screen, and an angle: 0° is straight down, about 52–58° is the "3D" look, 86° is side-on. Tilted views sit to the south-west, looking the way Northwest weather usually arrives. Named places include Green Lake & Seattle, Puget Sound, the Strait, the Alenuihāhā Channel, Kona, Hilo, Galveston Bay and central Houston. Home is the area's opening view: Green Lake & Seattle 3D for KATX and KATX+KLGX, Central Houston 3D for the Houston areas, the whole area in 3D for other merged live areas, and Focus 3D otherwise.

👁 Stand here puts the camera at the focus or a radar, at a height from eye level (2 m) to 40 km (presets: ground, hilltop 300 m, light plane 3 km, airliner 10 km, above the storm tops 25 km). While standing, heights are true (exaggeration 1×), the lens zooms 1–10×, and only labels within 40 km show. It first faces where weather usually comes from: 225° in the Northwest and Houston, 60° in Hawaiʻi.

Play and loop

A case starts playing as soon as two frames' data are in. Live opens in Fast 2D, which plays once its loop is in (a second or two), then moves to 3D once the whole 3D loop has loaded; in 3D alone (TIAH, or 3D chosen) the newest scan stays still until most of the loop is in (60% of the first radar's volumes, or after 90 s). Add ?play=0 to a link to open paused.

Live keeps a loop of 30, 60 or 90 minutes. The default is 60 min for one radar and 30 min for a merged area, and 30 min on a phone (switching area keeps the loop you had unless the new default is shorter), because every volume is held in memory. Single-radar cases include the radar's ~3-minute low-level updates as extra frames; untick 3-min low level in the time bar to step only through full volumes.

Fast 2D first, then 3D

A full radar volume (Level II) is about 5 MB and lands in the public archive only when the radar has finished it: about 4.5 minutes after it starts in precipitation mode, 7–10 in clear air (the 3D view shows the newest one as it scans). The NWS radar software also publishes small products for the lowest (0.5°) tilt alone: N0B reflectivity (0.5 dB steps, 0.25 km bins every 0.5°, out to 460 km, ~150 KB) and N0G radial velocity (0.5 m/s steps, out to 300 km, ~95 KB). They land about 80 seconds after the scan starts, and in storms each extra low-level (SAILS) scan comes as its own file, so the lowest tilt refreshes every 1.5–2 minutes instead of once a volume.

How live opens:

  1. ⚡ 2D straight away. The loop of lowest-tilt scans loads in a second or two (about 1–2 MB for 30 minutes) and plays, looking straight down on the whole area. Only dBZ and Vel; the other fields are greyed out.
  2. 3D behind it. The full volumes download in the background. The 3D button (Where → Time) counts them in (“3D 3/6”) and turns green when the whole 3D loop is in.
  3. Then it switches to 3D by itself, on the nearest time, tilting into the usual 3D view (if you've moved the camera, it stays where you put it).
  • Press ⚡ 2D to stay in 2D: it won't switch again (the 3D button still goes green, so you can switch when you like). A link made then carries fast=1 and opens the same way.
  • Press 3D any time: if the volumes aren't all in yet, it switches as soon as they are.
  • Load 3D in background (on by default, remembered on your device): off means 2D only, with no volume downloads at all, for a phone or a slow connection; press 3D to load them anyway. Link form bg3d=0.
  • The two loops have different frames (2D has every low-level scan, 3D one per volume); switching lands on the nearest time.
  • The sheet takes the field's colours and the threshold slider. In merged areas each point comes from the nearest radar that reaches it. It lies flat over the map (not at beam height), so read it as a map of the 0.5° tilt: its height grows with range, about 1 km up at 60 km out and 3 km at 150 km (see beam height).
  • TDWRs (TIAH) publish no such products here: that area opens straight in 3D.
When to stay in 2D

On a phone or a slow connection, or when you want the very latest low-level picture (a squall line coming ashore, showers crossing the Sound). For structure (how tall, is it rotating aloft, where the melting layer is), let it go to 3D.

The ⓘ Tips panel

The ⓘ Tips button opens a panel with four tabs: Situation (the chosen preset's advice), Reading this field (a short primer on the field on screen), Empty spaces (why parts of the volume are blank), and This case. That last tab does live beam arithmetic for your focus: how far it is from the radar, the lowest tilt's bottom, centre and top heights there, the beam width, the frame's VCP and tilt list, and, for a low-level update frame, what is new in it.

Exports (GIF and MP4)

⬇ Export makes a file of the picture with its time line, colour keys, labels and data credits, entirely in the browser. Choose what to record:

  • Loop playing: every frame of the loop at the playback speed (incomplete volumes left out; low-level frames left out if that box is unticked). The last frame is held an extra 1.5 s.
  • Circle: one orbit round the view's centre (60 steps for a GIF, 120 for an MP4).
  • Tour: flies between your tour stops (each leg eased over 2.5 s, then held 1.2 s). Needs at least two stops.
  • Fly the line: along the Fly-through line at its height and duration, looking ahead.

For Circle, Tour and Fly the line, Radar loop plays during the move (on by default) advances the radar frames at the playback speed as the camera moves; off holds the frame on screen. The live Fly-through and tour have the same choice as Loop plays while flying.

Every frame's data is prepared first. Before recording, the exporter loads the radar field for every frame the file will show (and builds derived fields such as HCA and SRV), plus satellite images and the wide-area satellite when those are on, with a progress note. If it cannot have everything within about three minutes, it records nothing rather than a file with gaps. While recording, each frame also waits for rain totals, NWS storms and the model's hour to settle. Wind particles are advanced by the exporter so they move in the file.

Format: MP4 uses the browser's own H.264 encoder (WebCodecs) and is far smaller than GIF, which matters for flights; where WebCodecs is missing it records in real time, as WebM if the browser cannot do MP4. Widths: 800, 1280 or 1920 px (never more than your screen's pixels).

On a phone

At 760 px wide or less the controls become a bottom sheet that opens folded, so the picture comes first. Tap a tab to open it; tap the open tab again to fold it. The time bar rides on top of the sheet. Opening a chart in the dock folds the sheet. Live loops default to 30 minutes and open in ⚡ 2D (untick Load 3D in background to stay in 2D with no volume downloads), and the satellite's visible band is read only for the newest frame unless you choose Visible.

2How radar sees

Most confusion about radar comes from picturing it as a camera that photographs a block of sky. It is not. It is a spotlight on a turntable: a narrow beam, spun in circles, one angle at a time. Everything in this section follows from that.

Scanning, tilts and VCPs

A WSR-88D (NEXRAD) sends out pulses and listens for echoes. Time to the echo gives the range; strength gives reflectivity; the phase shift from pulse to pulse gives the radial velocity; comparing horizontal and vertical pulses gives the dual-polarization fields. The antenna turns a full circle at one elevation angle (a tilt or sweep), steps up, and turns again. The set of tilts and speeds is the volume coverage pattern (VCP), shown on every frame. A full volume takes roughly 5–6 minutes.

  • Split cuts. The low tilts are scanned twice back to back: once for long-range reflectivity, once for Doppler velocity. The code keeps, for each field and each angle (to 0.1°), the sweep with the most valid gates, which picks the right half of each split cut automatically.
  • SAILS. VCPs 215 and 212 repeat the lowest tilt in the middle of the volume (MESO-SAILS does it twice). Single-radar cases turn each repeat into its own frame, about 3 minutes after the volume began, labelled low-level update. Only the lowest tilt is new in it; everything above repeats the volume before. Live mode grids each volume file whole, so it has no separate low-level frames.
  • AVSET skips the high tilts when no storm is tall enough to need them, so the top of the volume varies from scan to scan.
  • Lowest tilt differs by radar (as configured in the code; the frame's own tilt list is the truth): 0.5° at KATX, KRTX, KOTX, PHKM, PHWA, KHGX and KLCH; 0.3° at KCRP; 0.2° at KLGX and KPDT; 0.1° at the TIAH terminal radar. A lower first tilt means seeing closer to the surface far out.

Each frame's tilt list is in the Tips panel's This case tab and in the Beam & sweep tilt menu.

Beam height: why the radar misses the bottom

A beam leaves the antenna in a straight line, but the Earth curves away beneath it, and the atmosphere bends the beam slightly back down. The standard way to handle both, used by the radar's own products and by every calculation here, is the 4/3-Earth model: pretend the Earth's radius is 4/3 of its true 6,371 km (so R′ ≈ 8,495 km) and the beam is straight. Then the height of the beam centre at slant range r and elevation e is:

h = √( r² + R′² + 2·r·R′·sin e ) − R′ + hantenna

The code also uses the equivalent form by ground distance s: (R′ + h) = (R′ + hant) · cos e / cos(e + s/R′). Heights grow roughly with the square of range, so the blind layer under the beam deepens quickly.

The same arithmetic for KATX's 0.5° beam (antenna 195 m), with the beam's half-power edges at ±0.475°:

On the water

Nothing below the bottom edge is seen. At Shilshole that is the lowest ~1,400 ft: exactly where waterspouts, gust fronts, fog and the wind you sail or row in live. The radar sees the parent shower above; you infer the rest.

Beam width and beam filling

The WSR-88D beam is 0.95° wide between its half-power points. Its width is 2·r·tan(0.475°), about 17 m for every km of range: roughly 900–960 m at Shilshole, 1.7 km at 100 km, 3.8 km at 230 km. Everything inside that cone at one range gate is blended into one number.

  • A feature smaller than the beam is averaged with whatever surrounds it. A waterspout, tens of metres wide, is never resolved; its parent shower, and sometimes the shower's rotation, is.
  • A thin layer of rain that only partly fills a wide, distant beam reads weaker than it is (partial beam filling).
  • Far out, the beam straddles the melting layer and both rain and snow at once, which confuses reflectivity and the dual-pol fields.

The cone of silence

Above the highest tilt, near the radar, no beam passes at all. The gap is a funnel over the antenna: with a 10° top tilt, the top edge of the beam is about 2 km up at 10 km out and about 3.9 km up at 20 km (4/3-Earth, KATX's antenna). The top tilt varies with the VCP and with AVSET; the highest angle in each frame's tilt list is the one that sets the cone. In 3D it shows as an empty funnel; in an along-beam cross-section it cuts the tops off storms close to the radar.

Terrain blockage

Mountains stop beams. The blockage maps were computed per radar and per tilt from AWS Terrarium elevation tiles (zoom 10, about 100 m), with the method of Bech et al. (2003): treat the beam's cross-section as a disc of radius r·tan(beamwidth/2); terrain rising above the beam centre covers a circular segment of it; the blocked fraction is that segment's share of the disc. Blockage only accumulates outward: once a ridge has cut a beam, every gate beyond is at least that blocked (cumulative beam blockage).

  • Above about 10% blocked, reflectivity behind the obstacle reads low. NWS precipitation algorithms correct up to about 50%.
  • Above about 50%, the tilt is effectively blind there and higher tilts must stand in.

Switch on Layers → Beam blockage to shade the ground for the chosen tilt: clear under 10%, then yellow to red to dark red. In Beam & sweep, the beam itself is coloured by how blocked it already is (light blue under 10%, yellow 10–50%, orange 50–90%, dark red above) and stops where it is fully blocked. The Olympics shadow the coast and western Strait for KATX (one reason KLGX was built on the coast); the Cascades shadow the east; Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa block Kohala's view of the windward slopes.

The newest volume, as it scans

The archive gets a volume only once the radar has finished it. But NOAA also publishes each volume as it is being scanned: about 55 pieces, one every ~5 seconds, in the order the radar collects them, lowest tilt first. Joined together they are the archive file, byte for byte.

So in 3D the explorer follows the newest volume piece by piece (checking every 10 seconds) and regrids it as its tilts come in (every six new pieces, at most every 20 seconds, and once more when it's complete). In a test, a KATX volume that began scanning at 00:31:14Z was on screen with its 0.5° tilt at 00:31:31Z, 17 seconds later; the archive would have had it only after 00:35.

  • While it fills in, the frame says IN PROGRESS with the tilts so far. Its low levels are real and current; above the highest tilt so far it is simply empty (not clear air), so don't read echo tops from it until it's whole.
  • Wind and QVP profiles are added once the volume is whole. When the finished volume then appears in the archive, it isn't downloaded again.
  • A volume that was already whole when the page first saw it comes from the archive as usual; one whose pieces stop arriving for 2 minutes is handed back to the archive.
  • Merged areas follow each radar's volume in progress; each radar's part fills in as its pieces arrive.

Reflectivity (dBZ)

What it measures: how much of the pulse comes back. For raindrops it scales with drop diameter to the sixth power, so a few big drops outweigh a cloud of small ones. It is expressed on a log scale: dBZ = 10·log10(Z).

Rough guide (from the app's own notes): 20 dBZ light rain, 35 moderate, 45+ heavy shower rain or graupel. In the Rain totals layer the conversion to rain rate is explicit (§9).

Read the shape in 3D. A tall, narrow core is convection: a shower with an updraft. A flat sheet with a brighter layer near 1–2 km is stratiform rain with a melting-layer bright band: wet snowflakes look like huge raindrops to the radar. Use Surface mode at 35 dBZ to see cores, Max from above to see a composite.

Pitfalls
  • Echo on a clear day over water is often sea clutter or anomalous propagation, when warm air over cold water bends the beam down onto the waves (summer afternoons and fair-weather evenings). Check CC before believing it.
  • Bird and insect blooms rise around sunset as rings of weak echo.
  • Behind terrain it reads low; far out the beam overshoots shallow rain and drizzle entirely.
  • The bright band exaggerates rain in a ring round the radar.
On the water

Post-frontal showers over Puget Sound are compact, fast-moving cells. Their heavy cores are where the gusts come from; a tall core that then collapses pushes a gust front out ahead of the rain. Follow one core through the loop: its track and speed give its arrival time at your spot.

Radial velocity (Vel)

What it measures: only the part of the wind blowing toward the radar (green, negative) or away from it (red, positive). Wind crossing the beam at right angles reads zero, so a uniform wind looks like a green half and a red half with a grey zero line between. 1 m/s is 1.94 kt.

  • Rotation shows as a tight green–red pair side by side across the beam: a couplet. Looking out from the radar, inbound on the left and outbound on the right is counter-clockwise (cyclonic in the Northern Hemisphere). The Shilshole case at 22:22 UTC shows exactly that: outbound just SW of Shilshole beside inbound to its east, seen from KATX to the north.
  • Convergence or divergence is a pair lined up along the beam.
  • The zero line bends with height when the wind turns with height (veering or backing).

The Nyquist limit and folding

A pulsed Doppler radar measures speed from the phase change between pulses, and phase wraps round every 360°. The highest speed it can measure unambiguously is the Nyquist velocity VN; the app's notes put it at about 25 m/s (49 kt) for the low tilts here. A faster wind folds: it reappears at the opposite end of the scale.

measured = true − 2·VN · round( true / 2VN )

Unfolding in cases. Case volumes are dealiased with Py-ART's region-based method before gridding. An isolated patch of the wrong colour can still be a dealiasing error.

Unfolding in live mode (reference-wind method). Each live volume's VAD profile (§6) is fitted first, from raw velocities, by a method that does not care about folds. That profile then serves as the reference: at every gate, the wind the profile predicts along that beam is

vref = cos(e) · ( u·sin(az) + v·cos(az) )

and the reading is moved by whole multiples of 2VN to sit nearest it: v′ = v + 2VN·round((vref − v) / 2VN). The profile is interpolated linearly between fitted heights, carried up to 1.5 km above the top fit and 1 km below the lowest, and not across a gap wider than 2 km; with fewer than three fitted heights there is no reference and the velocities stay raw. This fixes folding wherever the real wind is close to the ring-average wind; it cannot fix a small, intense circulation that differs from the average by more than VN.

Pitfalls
  • With little echo (clear air, isolated showers) there may be no VAD profile, so live velocities stay raw. A sharp red/green boundary in strong wind may then be folding, not rotation. Check that the colours make sense against the wind.
  • In a merged area each point's colour is motion toward or away from the radar that owns that point, so colours change meaning across the boundary between radars.
  • Velocity is drawn with nearest-neighbour sampling in 3D: blending with the empty "no data" code at echo edges would invent strong speeds.

Storm-relative velocity (SRV)

A shower moving at 25 kt paints everything ahead of it outbound and behind it inbound, which hides small circulations. SRV subtracts the storm's motion, so what is left is the flow inside the storm, and a rotating updraft stands out as a tight inbound/outbound pair across the beam.

How storm motion is chosen. By default (auto) it is the mean of the VAD wind at every fitted height between 1 and 6 km, from the VAD profile nearest this frame in time (within 4 minutes); it needs at least three heights. Otherwise, or by choice, set it by hand as a direction from and a speed in knots (default from 240° at 25 kt). The panel says which is in use, e.g. "Removing 25 kt from 240° (mean 1–6 km VAD wind (12 heights))".

What is subtracted: for each point, the storm motion's component along the horizontal line from its owning radar: vSRV = v − (us·sin az + vs·cos az). The beam's tilt is ignored (cos e ≈ 1 at the low tilts that matter), so SRV reads best near the radar's low tilts. With no motion known, SRV shows plain velocity.

Waterspouts

Look for a small couplet (a few km or less) under a shower base in SRV. The beam is too wide to see the spout itself, and at Shilshole the lowest beam bottom is ~1,400 ft up. Hand-set the motion when a cell moves differently from the mean wind (cells on a boundary, right-movers).

Differential reflectivity (ZDR)

What it measures: the ratio of horizontal to vertical reflectivity, in dB. Big raindrops fall flattened like hamburger buns and read +2 to +4 dB. Tumbling hail, graupel and dry snow look round on average and read near 0.

  • ZDR ≥ 2 dB means large, flattened drops: typical under shower cores and at the leading edge of cells.
  • Big ZDR with modest reflectivity: a few large drops, an edge or a new cell.
  • High reflectivity with near-zero ZDR in a core: graupel or hail rather than rain.
  • A ZDR column, a finger of positive ZDR poking above the freezing level, marks an updraft strong enough to carry liquid drops into cold air: one of the best signs of an active, growing shower.

ZDR is hidden where the echo is too weak for it to mean anything (§4). TIAH, the terminal radar, has no dual-pol, so ZDR and CC are empty in its area.

Correlation coefficient (CC, ρhv)

What it measures: how alike the targets in the beam are from pulse to pulse, between the two polarizations. Pure rain or pure snow is very uniform (above 0.97). A mix (wet melting snow, rain with hail) drops to about 0.85–0.95. Non-weather targets (birds, insects, sea or ground clutter, smoke, tornado debris) fall below about 0.8.

The default view shows only the low values (≤ 0.93). In widespread rain the melting layer appears as a ring-shaped shell round the radar, because every beam crosses the layer at a different range; the shell's height is the melting level. The snow level on the slopes is usually about 300 m below the bright-band peak (the Melting level situation's note).

Tracing each voxel back along the beam. Rather than spreading gates outward, the code asks, for every voxel: which beam, leaving this antenna, would pass through me? In the 4/3-Earth frame, a voxel at ground distance s and height h above the antenna sits at central angle θ = s/R′, so

dx = (R′ + h)·sin θ    dy = (R′ + h)·cos θ − R′
slant range = √(dx² + dy²)    elevation = atan2(dy, dx)    azimuth = atan2(x, y)

In a merged area, a radar that is not at the grid centre gets each column re-expressed in its own frame through latitude and longitude first.

  1. Sweep choice. For each field, one sweep per elevation angle (rounded to 0.1°): the one with the most valid gates. That handles split cuts and SAILS repeats.
  2. Horizontal sampling. Within a sweep, the nearest ray in azimuth (no further than 1.1° away) and the nearest range gate.
  3. Vertical rule. A voxel is filled only if its elevation lies between the lowest tilt minus half a beamwidth and the highest tilt plus half a beamwidth. Within half a beam of a tilt (below the lowest, or above the highest), it takes that tilt's value. Between two tilts it is interpolated linearly in elevation angle; if one of the two has no echo there, the other is kept only if the voxel is within half a beamwidth (0.475°) of it.
  4. Velocity is nearest-tilt. Averaging two velocities (either of which may be folded) makes a value neither tilt measured, so velocity takes the nearer tilt instead of interpolating.

A voxel no beam came near stays empty. That is the point: the cone of silence and the wedge under the lowest tilt appear as the holes they are, instead of being smoothed over.

Byte encoding

Every voxel is one byte. 0 means no data; 1–255 spans the field's range linearly:

code = clamp( round( (value − lo) / (hi − lo) × 254 + 1 ), 1, 255 )

So one step is 100/254 ≈ 0.39 dB for reflectivity, 100/254 ≈ 0.39 m/s for velocity, 8/254 ≈ 0.03 dB for ZDR and 0.55/254 ≈ 0.002 for CC. Values beyond the range are clamped to its ends. Case files are gzipped per field per frame; live frames are encoded the same way in memory.

Weak-echo masking for dual-pol

Dual-pol values from weak returns are mostly receiver noise, and noise has low correlation, so an unmasked CC view is a fog of fake "non-weather". A flat cut would also throw away the light snow aloft near the radar that QVPs rely on. The cut here rises with range the way the radar's sensitivity falls:

ZDR and CC kept only where dBZ ≥ −31.5 + 20·log10(range in km)

That is about −11.5 dBZ at 10 km, +2.5 dBZ at 50 km and +8.5 dBZ at 100 km. Each dual-pol gate is judged against the reflectivity of its own sweep. The constant is nominal (roughly 10 dB above the radar's noise level), not calibrated per site. The same rule applies to the QVP's ZDR and CC.

Merged multi-radar areas: the owner map

Two radars' values are never blended. Instead, once per area, an owner map gives every voxel to one radar (or none), from geometry alone:

  1. A radar is a candidate for a voxel if its beams pass through it: elevation between its lowest tilt minus half a beam and 10° plus half a beam, and within the area's echo range (230 km; 460 km for the two 600 km storm-scale areas).
  2. Its beam there must be less than 50% blocked by terrain (at the nearest precomputed tilt).
  3. Among candidates, the nearest (smallest slant range) wins: nearer means a lower, narrower beam.

A merged frame is then each radar gridded into only the voxels it owns. Frames follow the first radar's volumes; each other radar contributes its volume nearest in time, within 4 minutes. If a partner radar had no scan within 4 minutes, its part of that frame stays empty and the time line says so. Because each voxel comes from one known radar, velocity at every point is radial to that radar (the Radar field shows which).

Vertical exaggeration

Weather is thin: a 200 km wide area and a 12 km deep sky. At true scale showers look like smears. The page therefore opens at ×2, with the terrain relief set to "relief = radar" so terrain is stretched by the same amount. Why ×2 and matched: at ×3 Mount Rainier looked too steep, and an older mismatch (radar ×5, terrain ×2) misled about where beams meet ridges. With the two matched, a beam visibly hits the ridge that blocks it.

  • The slider runs 1× to 12×. Balloon, model levels, focus-pole marks and satellite cloud tops all follow the radar's exaggeration.
  • Terrain relief can be set on its own (1×, 1.5×, 2×, 3×) but never above the radar's; when the two differ a note warns that rain and model levels then sit higher over hills than they really are.
  • Situation presets deliberately use more (3× to 6×) where structure matters more than realism.
  • Standing (👁) and flying drop to 1×, true heights, until you leave.
  • The focus pole's altitude marks are in your chosen units, labelled with the standard-atmosphere pressure for that height (a guide to "which level", not the day's actual pressure).

Rendering: ray-marching, threshold and opacity

The grid is uploaded to the GPU as a 3D texture. For every pixel, a ray is marched through the box in steps (0.8 of a voxel normally, 0.35 in High quality, which also renders at your screen's full pixel ratio). At each step the byte is looked up in a 256-entry transfer table that gives a colour and an opacity:

shown? = value ≥ thr (dBZ, ZDR) · |value| ≥ thr (Vel, SRV) · value ≤ thr (CC)
alpha = opacity × (0.12 + 0.88 × strength)

where strength rises from 0 at the threshold to 1 further into the field's range (for dBZ, (v − thr)/max(5, 60 − thr)). So the slider sets what appears and the opacity sets how much each shown value adds.

  • Volume: emission and absorption, front to back, corrected for step length; the ray stops once 97% opaque. A glowing cloud.
  • Surface: the first shown sample is drawn as a lit surface, coloured by the strongest value a little way inside it so cores still show. Best for storm shape.
  • Max: the strongest shown value along the ray (lowest for CC, furthest from zero for velocity): a 3D composite.

Terrain is opaque: a ray stops where it meets the ground as drawn. Reflectivity is sampled with smooth (linear) filtering; every other field uses nearest-voxel sampling so that "no data" is never blended into a value. The cutaway and slab simply restrict which samples the ray may use.

5Holes and limits

The empty spaces are real. Each voxel is traced back along the beam, and only voxels a beam actually passed through are drawn. The Tips panel's Empty spaces tab summarizes them; here is the full list.

  • Under the lowest tilt. The beam climbs with range and the Earth curves away, so the radar is blind to the lowest few hundred metres over most water, and to kilometres far out (table).
  • Cone of silence. The empty funnel over the radar, above the top tilt.
  • Top of the scan. The highest tilt depends on the VCP, and AVSET skips high tilts when no storm needs them. Storm tops near the radar can be cut off.
  • Between the high tilts. Upper tilts are spaced more widely than a beam. Where both neighbouring tilts have echo the grid interpolates between them; dashed lines in the cross-section show where the beams actually were.
  • Behind terrain. Blocked beams read low (over ~10%) or see nothing (over ~50%).
  • Range. Far out the beam is high and wide: shallow rain, drizzle and low cloud vanish, and whatever is seen is averaged over kilometres.
  • Merged gaps. Voxels that no radar sees well (owner "none") stay empty; a partner radar with no scan within 4 minutes leaves its share empty for that frame.
  • Partial volumes. A live volume with far fewer tilts than the rest (a restart or a cut-short scan: fewer than 60% of the median tilt count, or fewer than 3) is flagged incomplete, skipped in playback, and never chosen as "latest", but stays reachable on the slider.
  • Live lag. The newest volume is shown while it is still being scanned: its low tilts are real and current, its upper tilts are missing until they arrive (the frame says IN PROGRESS and how many tilts it has). A complete volume is 4.5–10 minutes old by the time it's whole. "3 min ago" means the volume's start time.
  • Velocity folding. Cases are dealiased with Py-ART; live velocities are unfolded only against the VAD reference wind, and stay raw where no profile could be fitted.
  • Dual-pol masked where echo is weak; none at all from TIAH.
  • C-band TIAH attenuates behind heavy rain: echo beyond a strong core can be missing or weak.

When a radar is down

NEXRADs go off the air for maintenance (days, occasionally weeks) or stop uploading. Live keeps the newest volumes by count, so without a check a down radar would just play its last scans. Each time a radar's bucket is checked (every 2 minutes), the page notes its newest volume; if that is more than 20 minutes old (a volume every 4.5–10 minutes, archived when the radar finishes it; the ⚡ 2D lowest-tilt files count too), an orange line under the title says so: “KATX: no new scan since 1:05 PM (2 h ago). The radar may be down; what's shown is its last scans.” The newest of the two sources counts: the 3D volumes (while they are loading) and the ⚡ 2D lowest-tilt files, so with Load 3D in background off the check still runs on the 2D files alone. With no volumes at all today or yesterday (UTC), it says that instead of waiting; with only the 2D files to go on it says “no scans in the last n min” (the loop plus 4 minutes is all it looks at). In a merged area, a down partner's part of each frame is labelled “down: no data” rather than “not in yet”, in 3D and in ⚡ 2D alike (in 2D a partner with no lowest-tilt scan within 4 minutes of a frame is otherwise labelled as a gap there).

A button offers a neighbouring area of the region without the down radar (same group first, single radars first), for example Show KLGX (Langley Hill) when KATX is down. Nothing switches by itself, so you always know which radar you are looking at. A radar that goes off the air mid-scan leaves a short last volume: the real-time follower waits 2 minutes for more pieces, then leaves it to the archive, where it shows as an incomplete volume (few tilts) that playback skips; the warning follows once the last scan is 20 minutes old. (KLGX did exactly this on 2026-09-29 at 00:46Z, stopping 16 pieces into a 79-piece volume.) Check the frame's “min ago” before trusting any live picture.

QVP: quasi-vertical profile

At a steep tilt every range gate maps to one height, and averaging the full 360° ring smooths away horizontal detail. What remains is how each field changes with height; stacking one profile per volume in time turns layers into bands you can follow for hours. The method is from Ryzhkov et al. (2016).

  1. Tilt: the one nearest 10°, among tilts of at least 6° (a volume cut short by AVSET at 8° or a pattern topping at 6.4° still works; below 6° a "profile" is too wide a ring). A volume with no such tilt gives an empty column.
  2. Averaging: reflectivity and ZDR are averaged as power (converted to linear units, averaged, converted back to dB) so one strong gate is not diluted the way averaging decibels would; CC is averaged as is.
  3. Coverage rule: a height is kept only when at least 25% of the azimuths had a value there.
  4. Heights use the 4/3-Earth formula with the antenna height, on a 50 m ladder up to 10 km; the display shows 0–8 km. ZDR and CC use the weak-echo mask first.

Live mode builds a QVP for every radar in the area as volumes arrive (the dock's radar menu picks which). A case has the one built for its first radar, over a window wider than its 3D frames: layers need hours to read. The yellow line marks the frame on screen.

Read it: a bright band in dBZ and a dip in CC mark the melting level; a rising bright band means warming (snow level up, more runoff), a falling one means the cold air is arriving. It works best in widespread (stratiform) rain; scattered showers make it patchy.

VAD winds

Take a ring of gates at one slant range on one tilt. If the wind is uniform across the ring, a radar looking round the circle sees it as a sine wave in azimuth:

vr = cos(e) · ( u·sin(az) + v·cos(az) ) + a0

The explorer's method (the same in Python for cases and JavaScript for live):

  1. Heights: every 0.25 km from 0.25 to 8 km above sea level.
  2. Rings: for each height, every Doppler sweep (best-populated per 0.1°) whose ring at that height lies 8–50 km from the radar in ground distance; the ring nearest 25 km is tried first, then the next. Five gates across the ring's range (±2) are used, thinned evenly to at most 360 points.
  3. Coverage QC: at least 24 of the 36 ten-degree sectors must have data, and no gap wider than 60°.
  4. Alias-proof grid search: candidate winds u, v from −60 to +60 m/s in 2 m/s steps are scored by the mean of 1 − cos(π·(observed − model)/VN), which ignores whole folds; then refined ±2 m/s in 0.25 m/s steps.
  5. Least squares: the readings are unfolded against that best guess, and (a0, u, v) is solved by ordinary least squares.
  6. Fit QC: the ring is rejected if the RMS residual exceeds 3.5 m/s; the next candidate ring is tried.

TDWR files record no Nyquist velocity, so for TIAH the fit is plain least squares with no unfolding; a ring that does fold fails the RMS test and is left blank rather than given a wrong wind.

Display: the Winds dock shows a time–height chart of barbs in knots, pointing into the wind (half barb 5 kt, full 10, pennant 50), coloured by speed, with a right-hand axis in thousands of feet. Gaps are heights with too little echo, or too uneven a wind, for a clean fit. Layers → VAD wind arrows draws the current frame's profile as arrows over the radar at every other height, pointing where the wind blows (40 kt draws 20 km long).

Remember

It is one average wind for a ring 16–100 km across, not the wind at your spot. In a hurricane, a sea breeze or a convergence zone the ring is not uniform, so treat it as indicative. Winds veering (turning clockwise) with height mean warm advection; backing means cold advection.

Why the wind at the surface differs from the wind aloft

Friction with the water and land slows the air near the surface; a few hundred metres up, it barely feels the surface and blows faster, often turned 10–30° clockwise (in the northern hemisphere). How much of that faster air reaches you depends on mixing: eddies that carry parcels of air up and down. A parcel brought down from 500 m keeps most of its speed for a few seconds: that is a gust. So gusts are not random: they are samples of the wind higher in the stirred layer, and their ceiling is roughly the strongest wind in it.

Two things stir the air: mechanical turbulence (wind over rough ground, waves, hills, and the shear between slow air below and fast air above) and convection (air heated from below rises in plumes, and the air above sinks to replace it). Convection is the strong one: on a sunny afternoon over land, or when cold air moves over warmer water, the stirred layer can be 1–2 km deep. Without it (at night over land, or warm air over cold water) the stirred layer is shallow and the surface wind is decoupled from the wind aloft.

Potential temperature: the tool for seeing mixing

Air cools as it rises (lower pressure, it expands): 9.8 °C per km when dry. So raw temperature always falls with height in stirred air, which hides the pattern. Potential temperature θ removes that: it is the temperature a parcel would have if brought down (or up) to the 1000 mb level without heat added or lost, θ = T · (1000/p)0.286 in kelvin. Example: 10 °C at 850 mb is θ ≈ 297 K, warmer in θ than 20 °C at 1000 mb (293 K), so that upper air is lighter and sits stably on top.

  • θ constant with height: the air is well stirred (neutral): a parcel moved up or down finds itself the same as its surroundings, so nothing stops the stirring.
  • θ rising with height: stable: a parcel pushed up is colder and heavier than its new surroundings and sinks back. The faster θ rises, the more stable.
  • θ falling with height (only near the ground on a hot afternoon): unstable: plumes rise on their own.
  • An inversion (temperature itself rising with height) is a very steep jump in θ: a strong lid.

The parcel method: finding the mixed-layer top

Take the air at the surface, give it a little extra warmth (0.5 K, standing for the plumes a warm surface sends up), and lift it: it rises freely while it is warmer in θ than the air around it, and stops where the profile's θ catches up. That height is the mixed-layer top. It is the long-standing operational method for mixing height (after Holzworth, used in air-quality work; reviewed by Seibert and others in 2000), and it works best in the daytime or cold-advection cases that matter most for gusts.

  • The lid: if an inversion starts below that height, the mixed layer stops at the inversion base: eddies can't punch through warm air above.
  • The surface value is the warmest θ in the lowest 150 m, not just the lowest reading: a balloon's first point is the screen at the launch site, which at night can be a degree colder than the air 50 m up. On 2026-09-29 the Līhuʻe trade-wind balloon's lowest reading alone put the top at 90 m in a layer that was clearly stirred.
  • Stable: a surface-based inversion (temperature rising from the ground) means the stirred layer is only tens of metres deep. Shallow: a top under 300 m without one (the air above stable, θ rising steadily). In both, little of the wind aloft reaches the surface.
  • The gust ceiling: the strongest wind the balloon reported between the surface and the top: what eddies could bring down on the strongest gusts.

Where the lids come from here

  • Hawaiʻi, the trade-wind inversion (~1.5–2.5 km): air sinking in the subtropical high warms as it is compressed and dries; it caps the trade cumulus (their flat tops) and the stirred layer below. A low, strong inversion squeezes the trades and makes them gustier through the channels; the balloon line flags it, with how much drier the air above is.
  • Salish Sea summer, the marine inversion (a few hundred metres): warm air over the cold water; the sea cools the air next to it, making a shallow stable layer under warmer air. Light, smooth surface winds under stronger winds aloft, fog and stratus in the mornings.
  • Night over land, the radiation inversion: the ground cools and chills the air in contact with it. The morning balloons (12Z: 5 am Pacific in summer, 4 am in winter) often show it; by afternoon heating has usually erased it over land, but not over the water.
  • After a cold front: cold air over warmer water; no lid for a kilometre or two, deep stirring, showers and squalls.

The wind that can mix down now (VAD)

The balloon is twice a day; the radar measures the wind with height every volume (the VAD). The readout takes the strongest VAD wind from its lowest fitted level up to the balloon's mixed-layer top: the fastest air within reach of the eddies right now. With no usable balloon it looks up to 1.5 km and says so; with a stable or shallow layer it quotes the VAD's lowest level instead, as the wind waiting above the lid.

What the airports say: wind, gust and gust factor

The two airports nearest your focus give the surface side: wind and gust from their METAR (the “16016G24KT” group: 160° at 16 kt gusting 24), and the gust factor = gust ÷ mean. Airports report a gust only when peaks exceed the mean by about 10 kt, so no gust group doesn't mean perfectly smooth. As a rough guide: a gust factor around 1.3 is ordinary gusty mixing; 1.5 and up is strong mixing, squalls or showers passing. Gusting airports show e.g. “16G24” on their map markers.

Stability over the water: sea minus air

Over the water it is the sea that heats or cools the air from below, so the difference between the water and the air decides whether the air over it is stirred:

  • Sea warmer (+): the sea heats the lowest air, it rises, and the stirring reaches up to the lid: gusts close to the wind aloft, cumulus and showers if the air is moist. Typical after a front, and in autumn and winter when the water is warmer than the air.
  • Air warmer (−): the sea cools the lowest air, which stays put: a stable, shallow layer, the wind aloft skating over it. Smoother water, lighter wind, and fog if the air's dew point is above the sea temperature. Typical in spring and summer over the cold Salish Sea and Pacific.
  • Within ±1 °C: near neutral: the wind and the terrain do the stirring.

The dots are NOAA tide stations (CO-OPS) that measure both water and air temperature: harbour and pier gauges (Port Townsend, Port Angeles, La Push, Westport, Toke Point, Astoria, South Beach; Kawaihae, Kahului, Honolulu, Mokuoloe, Nawiliwili; Galveston Bay and the Texas coast). NDBC's offshore buoys would be better placed, but their data can't be read by a web page and relaying them would need a server; the tide stations are live, and in the Sound arguably closer to the water you sail. Hover a dot for the two temperatures and how old they are. Layers → 🌊 Sea − air; link seaair=1.

Putting it together: a reading routine

  1. Stability over the water (the dots, the readout): red, grey or blue near you?
  2. The mixed layer (the balloon line or the Skew-T's green band): deep (a kilometre or more), shallow, or stable? Under which lid, how high?
  3. The wind within reach (the VAD part): how strong, and from where?
  4. The surface check (airports): is the surface getting it? Gusts, gust factor.
  5. The radar: showers and squalls stir locally; a gust front or outflow can bring wind down anywhere, whatever the average profile says.

The readout's last phrase does this comparison when both sides are there: mixing brings the wind aloft down (gusty, or most of the wind aloft at the surface; with the gust ceiling), the surface wind is well below the wind aloft (little mixing, or sheltered sites), stable / shallow mixing (the wind aloft stays aloft), or the surface is as windy as the radar's profile or more (local effects: terrain, channels, sea breeze).

Worked examples (2026-09-29)

  • Salish Sea, 6 pm: the Quillayute balloon (12Z) showed only ~100 m of stirred air under a stable layer; the VAD had 16 kt at 300 m; Boeing Field was variable at 4 kt and Renton 10 kt; Port Townsend's water was 1.9 °C colder than the air (Port Angeles 4.2 °C). Everything agrees: a stable evening, the 16 kt above not reaching the water; expect lighter, smoother winds, with the stronger wind arriving where it's funnelled (Admiralty Inlet, the Strait's gaps).
  • Big Island, 3 pm: Līhuʻe's balloon (262 km away) had ~400 m stirred; the Kohala radar's VAD only 2 kt (clear air, few echoes); Upolu Point 14G19 (gust factor 1.4), Kona 9 kt; Kawaihae +0.6 °C (neutral). The surface is windier than the radar's average: the trades funnelling round Kohala, a local effect the VAD's ring round the radar can't see.
On the water

Unstable (sea warmer than the air), a deep mixed layer and strong wind aloft: gusts well above the mean, toward the VAD speed, often in bursts under the showers. Reef early, before the squall line arrives.

Stable (air warmer than the sea) and a shallow mixed layer: the surface wind is lighter than aloft and smoother, but it can arrive suddenly where the land funnels it down: off headlands, in the passes and gaps, and when a shower or afternoon heating breaks the lid.

Pitfalls and limits

  • Time: the balloon is up to 12 hours old (it flies at 00Z and 12Z: 5 am and 5 pm Pacific in summer, 4 in winter; 2 am and 2 pm in Hawaiʻi). A morning balloon's night-time inversion is often gone over land by afternoon, but not over cold water.
  • Place: one balloon, often 50–260 km away (Hawaiʻi: Hilo, in the area, when it flies; recently it hasn't, and Līhuʻe is used, which the balloon line says). The VAD is an average round a ring 16–100 km across the radar, and needs scatterers: patchy in clear air. Airports are over land. Tide stations measure shallow harbour water at one pier and miss the tidal stirring in the passes.
  • What it isn't: not a forecast. Nothing here is modelled or filled in: where a source is missing, the readout says so.

Like a medical CT, a slab of chosen thickness (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 5 or 10 nm) is stepped through the volume, and only what lies inside it is drawn. Modes:

8Nowcast (look ahead)

Radar → Look ahead slides the frame on screen forward by 5 to 60 minutes along the echoes' recent motion. It is extrapolated, not observed: nothing grows, fades, forms or dies. Honest for 10–30 minutes in organised rain, rough beyond, and wrong for new storms.

Motion

  1. The frame is paired with the most recent earlier full scan (not a low-level update, not incomplete) at least 3 minutes and no more than 20 minutes before it.
  2. Both volumes' reflectivity is collapsed to a column maximum on ~2 km cells.
  3. Blocks about 24 km across, every 12 km, of the newer picture are matched against the older one by sliding them around (least absolute difference), up to the distance a 150 km/h motion would cover. A block needs at least 8% of its cells at 20 dBZ or more to be tracked.
  4. Outliers (more than 2.5 median deviations from the median motion) are dropped. With three or more good blocks, the motion at any point is an inverse-distance blend of the block vectors with the echo-weighted mean as a weak background.
  5. With fewer than three, one mean motion is used; if there is none, the steering wind is used: the same storm motion as SRV (the 1–6 km VAD mean, or your hand-set motion).

Advection

Every column of the 3D volume is carried along that motion for the lead time, the whole column together, working backwards (each point takes the value from where its air was). Displacements are computed on a ~4 km lattice and interpolated per column. The nowcast applies to whichever field is on screen; the motion always comes from reflectivity.

Controls: ▶ plays +5 min every 0.4 s, pausing at +60, and round again; the radar loop stops while it plays (one clock at a time). ⇩ Prepare all steps works out +5 to +60 for the frame on screen so dragging is smooth (no download: it uses the scans already loaded). The info line gives the mean speed and direction, the two scan times used and how many areas were tracked, or "too few echoes: steering wind used". The top line reads NOWCAST +N min (extrapolated). In a case, step forward to the real frame to see how it did.

Try it

Clouds vs rain drapes the imagery at cloud-top height above the radar's rain. A tall, cold top over a tall core is an active shower; a cold top with little echo under it is anvil or cirrus; bright cloud with a warm top and no echo is low stratus or fog, which radar cannot see at all.

Fog and low stratus index

Computed in the browser for every frame (cases and live) from GOES bands 13 (10.3 µm) and 7 (3.9 µm), plus the visible when there is one. The info line says which test was used.

  • Night (sun more than 90° from overhead): small water droplets emit less at 3.9 µm than at 10.3 µm, so BT13 − BT7 is positive over fog and stratus, typically +2 to +5 K; ~0 over clear ground or water, negative under cirrus. The index ramps up from +1 to +3.5 K.
  • Day (sun within 80° of overhead): water-droplet tops reflect sunlight at 3.9 µm, so BT7 reads well above BT13 (the index ramps from 8 to 20 K); clear land does that too, so the cloud must also be bright in the visible (reflectance ramping from 0.18 to 0.35). With no visible image the day test uses IR cloudiness instead and is capped at 60%.
  • Twilight (80–90°): the two tests blended; least reliable.
  • Both: the top must be warm, within about 8–16 K of the surface temperature (i.e. low), and any column with radar echo of 15 dBZ or more in the lowest 2 km is cut to 15%: a cloud producing rain is not fog.

Teal shows where the index is 25% or more (brighter with higher values); other cloud shows faint grey. Hover for the index. Satellite cannot tell fog from low stratus whose base is off the water: the airport reports settle that.

Lightning (GOES GLM)

The Geostationary Lightning Mapper is an optical camera on the GOES satellite that sees lightning light up cloud tops, day and night. It reports each flash with time, position, energy and area; only flashes with a good quality flag are kept. It counts total lightning, in-cloud and cloud-to-ground alike, which is what matters on the water: in-cloud flashes come first and signal a charging storm. Off by default (Layers → ⚡ Lightning): live, it means about 30 MB of GOES files for a loop. Switch it on before relying on the lightning badge below; the Lightning situation preset switches it on for you.

  • Every flash from the start of the loop up to the end of the frame's volume is drawn, so they build up as the loop plays; colour by age: newest white-yellow, then orange, oldest red.
  • The badge at the top counts flashes within 20 nm of the focus over the loop and in the last 10 minutes, the nearest in the last 10 minutes (nm), and the total in the area. It highlights when a flash in the last 10 minutes was within 10 nm.
  • Live: the bucket is checked every minute (a file every 20 s, ~220 KB each: about 30 MB for a 45-minute window). Files download only while the ⚡ Lightning layer is on: switch it off and the feed pauses; switch it on again and it fetches just the files it hasn't read yet. When a live area opens, lightning waits until the radar loop is in (or 45 s) and then reads three files at a time, so the radar gets the connection first; cases carry flashes from 10 minutes before to 10 minutes after the case. Files already read are kept for the visit (switching area doesn't read them again) and on this device for 2 hours (a reload doesn't either).
Limits

Pixels are about 10 km across here; positions are good to a few km. It sees light through cloud tops, so weak flashes low in thick cloud and small, shallow Northwest storms can be missed. It cannot separate cloud-to-ground from in-cloud. No flashes shown is not an all-clear; if you can hear thunder, you are within range.

Rain totals

🌧 Rain totals colours the ground by rainfall over a window ending at the frame on screen. The 3D volume steps aside while they show (tick 3D volume beside it to bring it back). The window menu labels each choice with the source it will actually use.

From this radar (the loop, or any window the loop covers)

  1. Lowest echo per column: for each grid column, the lowest voxel with echo within 3 km of sea level.
  2. Z–R relationship: reflectivity to rain rate, with Z = 10dBZ/10 and R in mm/h:
    Northwest (Marshall–Palmer): Z = 200 R1.6
    Houston and Hawaiʻi (NWS tropical): Z = 250 R1.2
    Tropical rain (many small-to-medium drops, warm clouds) gives more rain for the same reflectivity. Echo under 15 dBZ counts as no rain, and reflectivity is capped at 53 dBZ, as NWS precipitation algorithms do, so hail cores do not read as absurd rain.
  3. Accumulation: each frame's rate is applied for the time it stands for (until the next frame starts, or its recorded span), clipped to the window and to "now", and summed.

It updates every scan. Choosing a fixed window the loop already covers (within 5%) uses this radar too.

How good is it?

Typically within 30–50% of gauges, often worse: the beam is high far from the radar (it misses shallow rain and sees melting snow), terrain blocks it, and one Z–R cannot fit every storm. Treat totals as a pattern (where the heavy rain fell) more than a number.

MRMS totals (longer windows)

Windows of 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48 or 72 hours that the loop does not cover come from NOAA MRMS (Multi-Radar/Multi-Sensor), read straight from NOAA's public bucket, decoded in the browser (GRIB2 with PNG packing) and resampled to the nearest MRMS cell. Hawaiʻi uses MRMS's Hawaiʻi domain; the others CONUS.

  • Gauge-corrected ("MultiSensor Pass 2") is used when one ends within 2 hours before the frame. It is multi-radar with its bias scaled to the rain gauges that reported. It is not gauge totals: where the radar sees little, it stays low. It runs 1–2 hours behind, so the latest hour or two is not in it yet.
  • Radar-only is used otherwise (within 1.5 hours): fresher, not gauge-adjusted.
  • The info line names which, when it ends, how many minutes before the frame, and the maximum.
Hawaiʻi: windward under-count

The Big Island's radars see little of the windward slopes' rain: Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa block Kohala's beams, South Point's beams pass over it, and trade-wind rain is shallow.   • Wind as: Auto (streamlines at the surface, barbs aloft, since stacked streamlines tangle), Streamlines, Barbs, or ✦ Particles. Spacing (6–40 km, default 14) sets the distance between barbs or streamlines, never closer than 25 km for GFS.
  • Levels aloft as wire mesh draws each level in use as a faint mesh at its true height, so troughs dip and ridges bulge. Freezing level (0 °C) surface draws the model's own freezing level.
  • Smooth between model points on blends between grid points; off shows each model point's own value, so you see the model's resolution.

Wind particles

✦ Particles shows the wind on each level whose box is ticked (the ✓ beside each level in the list; with particles on, the list's heading and a note in the particle options say so) as drifting streaks in that level's colour (surface white, 925 cyan, 850 green, 700 yellow, 500 orange, 300 pink, 250 violet). The motion is sped up to read: about 10 m/s crosses 3% of the area a second, whatever the area's size. Faster wind draws longer and brighter streaks.

  • ✦ low + barbs (a Wind-as choice): particles on the surface and 925 mb levels, where the flow over the water and through the gaps matters, and barbs on the ticked levels above, so the boundary layer moves while the winds aloft stay readable. The same ✓ boxes decide which levels show.
  • Between levels (3D, interpolated): particles at every height from the lowest ticked level to the highest (a single ticked level gives a band round it; none ticked, none), each taking the wind interpolated linearly in height between the model levels above and below it, coloured by the level at or below. It loads every level. Smooth where the wind changes smoothly with height; it softens sharp shear (an inversion top, a front, a jet core). Particles keep their own height: the model's vertical motion is not used.
  • Density 0.25–12×, weighted to low levels (surface and 925 mb get more).
  • Low-level boost 1–4×: extra particles at the surface and 925 mb (in 3D, more of them near the ground), where the detail is: terrain channelling, sea breezes, gap winds.
  • Bright heads (a dot at the head of each streak), Size 1–5 px, Opacity.

At the highest densities particles can cost around 10 ms a frame and may stutter on a phone.

Comparing model with observation

The model is a consistent, gap-free picture of the whole air; the radar and balloon are real but partial. Use each for what it is good at:

  • Model winds vs VAD: hover a spot near the radar for the model's wind at each level and compare with the Winds dock at the same heights. Agreement builds trust in the model's steering flow; disagreement near the surface is common where terrain channels the wind.
  • Model freezing level vs radar: compare the model's 0 °C surface with the CC melting-layer ring or the QVP bright band (the band sits a little below 0 °C, where snow melts).
  • Model vs balloon: turn on both; the balloon's levels (spread across the area, as an assumption) against the model's. The balloon is truth at one point and time.
  • Check the valid time line: an HRRR analysis can lag the frame by up to an hour; GFS in Hawaiʻi is a forecast for the nearest hour.

The wide 2,000 km area

Air → Area → wide 2,000 km centres a 2,000 km square on the radar area: the scale of a hurricane or a front. 🗺 Map view flies straight down onto the whole area, north up.

  • Beyond the radar area the map comes from Natural Earth (public domain): land, coastlines, country and state borders, a 5° grid and the main places.
  • Wide satellite: IR (band 13, on ~5 km cells, about 3 MB a scan), visible (band 1, 1 km, daytime, about 14 MB per 10 minutes, one scan per 10 minutes), Auto (visible only if the sun is at least 15° up over the area's centre at every frame of the loop, else IR), or off. The 5-minute sector is used wherever it covers, and the 10-minute full disk fills only the rest (Hawaiʻi south of ~14.6°N, the Northwest north of ~52°N).
  • Wide satellite opacity has its own slider beside the band menu (0.1–1, default 0.85), separate from the radar-area satellite's. Lower it to let the model's colours, isobars and the map show through the clouds; it changes instantly, even while the loop plays.
  • While the wide area is on, the radar area's own ground lies flat and hidden, so the GFS fill, the wide satellite and the map run unbroken across it; it comes back when you return to the radar area.

14Assumptions and accuracy

Every picture here rests on assumptions. In one place:

  1. 4/3-Earth refraction for every beam height, the gridder, the section and the owner map. Real refraction varies: warm air over cold water can duct the beam down (sea clutter, anomalous propagation), so beams can be lower, or higher, than drawn.
  2. 0.95° beamwidth for all radars, including TIAH, whose real beam is narrower.
  3. Nearest ray (within 1.1°) and nearest gate horizontally; linear-in-elevation interpolation between tilts vertically (velocity nearest-tilt).
  4. One byte per voxel: steps of ~0.39 dB and ~0.39 m/s; values clamped to each field's range.
  5. Weak-echo mask for ZDR and CC with a nominal, uncalibrated sensitivity line.
  6. Velocity: Py-ART region dealiasing in cases (can err); in live, only reference-wind unfolding against the VAD (raw where none).
  7. VAD assumes a uniform wind round a 16–100 km ring; QC as described; heights MSL.
  8. SRV subtracts only the along-beam part of one storm motion and ignores tilt.
  9. Merged areas: owner map from geometry and a 50% blockage rule; partner volume within 4 minutes, not the same instant.
  10. HCA is the NWS algorithm's guess, placed at the nearest of four tilts within 0.5°.
  11. QVP assumes horizontal uniformity round the ring (power-averaged dBZ/ZDR, 25% coverage).
  12. Nowcast is pure advection of the whole column; no growth or decay.
  13. Rain from radar: one Z–R per region, lowest echo within 3 km, 15 dBZ floor, 53 dBZ cap; often ±30–50%.
  14. MRMS gauge-corrected is a bias scaling, not gauge totals; it under-counts where radar sees little (windward Big Island).
  15. Gauges: every-day and ≤25 in/day QC; CoCoRaHS 7 am day boundaries.
  16. Satellite cloud tops from ACHA plus a 6.5 K/km IR estimate; thin cirrus reads too low, stratus under an inversion near zero; parallax corrected with those heights.
  17. Fog index is a heuristic of smooth ramps; twilight least reliable; cannot see fog under higher cloud; cannot tell fog from stratus.
  18. Balloon spread planes assume one profile holds across the area.
  19. Model is a forecast system: analyses lag up to an hour; GFS is a forecast even at +0; particles use horizontal wind only.
  20. Standard-atmosphere pressure on the pole marks and level menu is a guide, not the day's value.
  21. Marine chart is simplified; not for navigation.

15Workflows on the water

These are ways to use what the tool shows. They are not forecasts and they do not replace NWS marine forecasts, warnings or your own eyes.

Pre-departure check (Puget Sound)

  1. Live, KATX (or KATX + KLGX for the Strait and coast), focus on your launch spot (save it with ☆).
  2. ⓘ Tips → This case: note the lowest beam height at your spot. That is how much of the low air you are blind to.
  3. dBZ, Max, Low levels, from above; play the loop. Where are showers, how are they moving? Try Look ahead +15 to +30 min, remembering it is extrapolated.
  4. Winds dock: the steering flow and any strong wind just above the surface layer.
  5. Lightning badge; airports for visibility and ceiling; Air → HRRR surface wind for the pattern, labelled as model.
  6. Then look at the sky and the water.

Reading showers over the Sound

Use What's coming? (Surface at 35 dBZ) and follow one core through the loop; its track and speed give arrival time. In the Northwest, showers move with the 700–500 hPa wind, commonly from the SW–W at 20–40 kt. A core that is tall and then collapses, or a bowed leading edge, pushes a gust front out ahead of the rain: the wind can shift and jump 10–25 kt minutes before the rain arrives, and radar rarely shows that gust front over water.

Spotting the Puget Sound Convergence Zone

When NW flow splits around the Olympics and rejoins over the Sound, look for a narrow W–E band of showers, often near the King–Snohomish county line, that stays in place or drifts north–south while the rest of the region is showery or dry. Use the Puget Sound Convergence Zone situation, then switch to Velocity (or Gap winds, fronts & convergence) to see the two flows meet: a sharp colour change along the beam. Under the band: heavy showers, gusty and shifting winds, sometimes thunder; either side can be light winds.

Gust fronts and outflow

Watch for collapsing tall cores and bowed edges in dBZ, and for a line of outbound (or inbound) velocity racing ahead of the rain near the radar, where the beam is low enough to catch it. Far from the radar the outflow is usually below the beam: assume it may be there before you see it.

Fog

Use the Fog & low stratus situation: teal satellite index, airport markers and low-level radar. Teal over IFR/LIFR airports with FG or BR means fog on the surface; teal over VFR with an 800–1,500 ft ceiling means low stratus. Step through time: teal shrinking from the edges after sunrise is burn-off; teal pushing inland through the Strait or up the Sound is a marine push. Over open water, between airports, trust what you see.

Waterspout clues

Use Rotation / waterspout check, then SRV. The classic set-up is a tall shower with a sharp edge, light winds and a cold airmass over warmer water. Look for a small couplet across the beam at the lowest tilts under a shower base, and ZDR or dBZ showing a compact, vigorous cell. The radar sees the parent shower at best; spouts live below the beam. The Shilshole case is the worked example.

Big Island: windward rain and the channel

Trade-wind showers & channel winds shows the acceleration through the Alenuihāhā Channel in velocity, as the radar sees it aloft (PHKM's lowest beam is 1–2 km above the channel). For rain, remember the windward under-count: compare Rain totals with Rain gauges on the Hilo, Puna and Hāmākua slopes before trusting any radar or MRMS number there. The balloon from Hilo shows the trade-wind inversion that caps the showers.

Houston convection and flooding

Start from Central Houston 3D on the satellite photo with Roads & waterways on (the bayous). Squall lines and fronts come from the west and north-west, Gulf storms and sea-breeze cells from the south. For flooding use Heavy rain & flooding: switch between 24 h (how wet the ground already is) and Loop (what is falling now), and watch several inches in a few hours over one bayou's watershed. TIAH (with KHGX as HOUSTON) adds low, close-in detail on the north side but attenuates behind heavy rain.

16Performance and data use

  • Live downloads: the code's own estimate is about 150–300 MB an hour for radar. Each volume is downloaded once, either piece by piece while it is scanned (the newest) or whole from the archive, and the next downloads while the current one is processed.
  • Hidden tab: when the tab is hidden (switched away, minimised, phone screen off), every live feed pauses: radar, lightning and airports. On return they re-list the bucket and fetch only what now falls inside the loop; the status line says "paused while hidden … catching up".
  • Only what is on: lightning, satellite, airports, rain, gauges, storms and the model fetch only while their layer is on, for the area on screen. Live visible satellite costs ~6–10 MB a scan, so it is read only when it would be shown; on a phone, only for the newest frame.
  • Memory: an unpacked volume is roughly 33 MB (a merged frame about twice that). Frame arrays not on screen are packed as runs of non-zero bytes (about 95% of voxels are empty sky), 10–20× smaller, and unpacked on first use; the field on screen stays unpacked so playback is instant. Secondary-radar volumes in merged areas stay packed until paired. The ⚡ 2D files are put onto the area's grid as they arrive and only that is kept (~170 KB a scan for KATX instead of ~1–1.3 MB decoded), so a long 2D loop stays small. In cases only the current field and the one before it are kept.
  • Nothing downloaded twice: changing the loop length (30 → 60 min) keeps what's loaded and fetches only the extra, older volumes (or drops the oldest). Downloaded volumes are also kept on your device for 2 hours (browser storage, up to 80 files), so switching between areas that share a radar (KATX ↔ KATX + KLGX) or reloading the page reuses them; only the gridding (a couple of seconds a volume) is redone. The newest volume, built from the real-time pieces, is stored the same way once whole. The ⚡ 2D files (2 hours) and lightning files (2 hours, and for the whole visit in memory) are kept the same way. The status line says reading from this device for these and downloading only when a volume really comes over the network (the download meter agrees). Switching area keeps your loop length unless the new area's default is shorter, so it doesn't fetch a stretch of older volumes you didn't ask for.
  • Fast 2D: the lowest tilt only, from Level III (~250 KB a scan for both fields per radar), about 20–40× less than the volumes; see Fast 2D. Live opens with it and loads the 3D volumes behind.
  • Radar first: when a live area opens, the radar volumes get the connection to themselves; lightning, when on (about 135 GLM files, ~30 MB for a 45-minute window), starts once the radar loop is in, or after 45 s, three files at a time. The loop starts playing once most of it is in (60% of the first radar's volumes) or all of it, or after 90 s; until then the newest scan stays on screen.
  • Live pipeline: each radar has its own worker. While one volume is decoded and gridded, the next one downloads, so there is no idle gap. A second worker per radar handles the volume in progress from the real-time pieces: each time new tilts arrive it updates only the field on screen, and only from just below the newest tilt up (velocity whole when the VAD wind that unfolds it has changed); the other fields are done once the volume is whole. Checked voxel for voxel against a full regrid: identical, for about 60% less CPU per volume. The field on screen is gridded first and shown as soon as it's ready; the other fields follow a moment later (about 0.5–1 s on a laptop), and in a merged area each partner radar's share is painted in when its fields arrive. Decoding and gridding a volume takes a few seconds of CPU; on most connections the ~5 MB download per volume is the slower part.
  • Download meter (top right, “↓ 150 MB”): everything this page has downloaded in the last 10 minutes, with a bar per minute (newest on the right). Hover it for the breakdown by source (radar 3D volumes, the newest volume as it scans, 2D lowest tilt, lightning, satellite, model, rain totals, IEM, this site, libraries) and the total since the page opened. It counts downloads in the page and in its background workers, as bytes on the wire; volumes reused from this device's store don't count. Watch it to see a slow line's budget, or what a layer costs: switch it on and see the next minute's bar.
  • What opening costs: the page itself is small: about 350 KB of scripts (minified), three.js from a CDN, and per area a satellite photo of 0.1–4 MB (WebP) plus terrain and lines. The marine chart, 0.1–1.8 MB compressed, loads only when that layer is on. Live data is the bulk: each radar volume is about 5 MB, so a 30-minute loop on one radar is ~30 MB in clear air; in precipitation mode volumes run ~7 MB and come more often, and KATX + KLGX came to ~100 MB of volumes on the meter, plus ~30 MB of lightning if you switch it on (it's off by default) and ~10 MB of satellite if it's on (about 150 MB in the first half-minute, measured 2026-09-29).
  • Phone defaults: 30 min live loop; controls folded; visible satellite for the newest frame only.
  • Model data: HRRR analyses read only the tiles over the area (~10 KB each); forecast fields ~0.5–0.9 MB each; Load all estimates ~4 MB per level-hour for HRRR and ~3 MB for GFS.
  • High quality renders at full pixel ratio with finer ray steps: prettier, slower.
On a slow connection

Live already opens in Fast 2D (a whole loop in about 1–2 MB); press ⚡ 2D to stay there, or untick Load 3D in background so no volumes download at all. For 3D, open a single-radar area (KATX rather than KATX + KLGX) and keep the 30-minute loop: that roughly halves the download. The newest scan appears first, within the time of one ~5 MB download, and the loop fills in behind it. Leave visible satellite and the wide area's visible imagery off (IR is far smaller), and leave Lightning off (the default) unless storms are about (its files download only while the layer is on). A hidden tab pauses everything, so nothing downloads while you're elsewhere.

17Glossary

ACHA
NOAA's GOES cloud-top height product, used for the 3D cloud-top surface.
Aliasing (folding)
A radial speed beyond the Nyquist velocity wrapping to the other end of the scale.
AVSET
A scan mode that skips high tilts when no storm is tall enough to need them.
Beam blockage
The fraction of a beam's power cut off by terrain; cumulative along each radial.
Bright band
A layer of enhanced reflectivity just below the 0 °C level, where melting snow looks like huge raindrops.
CC (ρhv)
Correlation coefficient: how alike the targets in the beam are. Low means mixed or non-weather.
Cone of silence
The unscanned funnel above the top tilt, near the radar.
Couplet
Adjacent inbound and outbound velocities; across the beam it means rotation.
dBZ
Reflectivity on a log scale, 10·log10(Z).
Dealiasing (unfolding)
Putting folded velocities back where they belong.
GLM
Geostationary Lightning Mapper, the optical lightning sensor on GOES.
HCA
Hydrometeor classification algorithm: the NWS's call on rain, snow, hail, clutter and so on.
HRRR / GFS
NOAA's 3 km hourly model over the contiguous US, and its global 0.25° model.
MRMS
Multi-Radar/Multi-Sensor: NOAA's national radar mosaic and rain analysis.
Mixed layer
The air stirred from the surface; gusts come from its upper part. Its top is usually an inversion.
Nyquist velocity
The highest radial speed a Doppler radar measures unambiguously.
Owner map
Which radar each voxel of a merged area comes from.
Gust factor
Gust divided by the mean wind at an airport; 1.4 and up means strong mixing or squalls.
Parallax
A satellite's slanted view placing high cloud tops away from the satellite on a flat map.
Potential temperature (θ)
The temperature air would have if brought to 1000 mb without heat added or lost: constant through a well-mixed layer, rising with height in stable air.
QVP
Quasi-vertical profile: a steep tilt averaged round the circle, stacked in time.
Radial velocity
Motion toward or away from the radar only.
SAILS
Supplemental adaptive intra-volume low-level scan: a mid-volume repeat of the lowest tilt.
Sea minus air
Water minus air temperature: + unstable (sea warmer), − stable (air warmer), within ±1 °C near neutral.
SRV
Storm-relative velocity: radial velocity minus the storm's motion.
TDWR
Terminal Doppler Weather Radar: the FAA's C-band airport radar (TIAH).
Tilt / sweep
One full turn of the antenna at one elevation angle.
Transfer function
The table turning each stored value into a colour and an opacity for rendering.
VAD
Velocity-azimuth display: a sine fit round a ring of gates giving the average wind at one height.
VCP
Volume coverage pattern: the set of tilts and speeds a radar runs.
Vertical exaggeration
Stretching heights so thin weather shows its shape; ×2 by default.
Voxel
One cell of the 3D grid.
Z–R relationship
Z = a·Rb, turning reflectivity into rain rate.
ZDR
Differential reflectivity: horizontal versus vertical reflectivity; big flattened drops read high.
